Introduction

When a language model answers a question, the visible part is tiny: a prompt goes in, text comes out. The invisible part is an industrial process that can consume thousands of accelerators for months and cost millions of dollars before the first user ever types a word. This artifact explains that process end to end and puts numbers on it, because an engineer who cannot reason about training cost cannot choose a model size, a data budget, or a deployment strategy responsibly.

The Training Pipeline

Reading the diagram: each stage feeds the next, and cost is wildly uneven across stages. Pretraining consumes the overwhelming majority of compute; fine-tuning and alignment consume the majority of human judgment.

The Stages, and What Each One Costs

1. Data Curation

What — Collecting web text, books, code, and licensed corpora, then deduplicating, filtering low-quality and toxic content, and removing personal information. Cost — Mostly engineering time and storage, not accelerator time, but it decides the ceiling of everything downstream: a model trained on polluted data cannot be fixed by more compute.

2. Tokenization

What — Splitting text into tokens, the model's actual input units, with a vocabulary of tens of thousands of pieces. Cost — Cheap to run, expensive to get wrong: a tokenizer that wastes tokens on common words inflates every later cost, since training cost scales with the number of tokens processed.

3. Pretraining

What — Training a transformer to predict the next token on trillions of tokens, using data parallelism across thousands of GPUs with high-bandwidth interconnects. Cost — The dominant cost of the whole pipeline, often 90% or more of total compute. Approximate training compute follows 6×N×D floating-point operations, where N is parameters and D is tokens.

4. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

What — Continuing training on curated instruction-response pairs so the model follows instructions instead of merely continuing text. Cost — A small fraction of pretraining compute, but a large share of human cost: high-quality instruction data is written and reviewed by people, and its quality caps the model's usefulness.

5. Alignment (RLHF / DPO)

What — Training a reward model from human preference rankings, then optimizing the model against it (RLHF), or optimizing preferences directly (DPO), plus safety evaluation and red-teaming. Cost — Moderate compute, heavy human and evaluation cost. This stage is where the model learns to refuse harmful requests, admit uncertainty, and behave consistently with its deployer's values.

6. Deployment and Inference

What — Serving the model behind an API or product, with batching, caching, quantization, and monitoring. Cost — Recurring rather than one-time: at scale, total inference cost overtakes training cost. Every token generated costs compute, so serving efficiency (smaller models, distillation, speculative decoding, KV-cache management) decides the product's margin.

Two published reference points make the scale concrete. GPT-3 (175 billion parameters, about 300 billion training tokens) was reported at launch as a multi-million-dollar training run. The Chinchilla study (Hoffmann et al., 2022) then showed that many models were undertrained: for a fixed compute budget, the compute-optimal balance is roughly 20 tokens per parameter, so a 70-billion-parameter model trained on 1.4 trillion tokens (Chinchilla itself) outperformed much larger models trained on less data. The practical consequence is that buying more parameters without proportionally more data wastes money, and the cheapest capability gain is often better data, not a bigger model.

Scaling Laws in One Paragraph

Scaling laws (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022) describe a smooth, predictable relationship: as compute, parameters, and data grow, loss falls as a power law, with diminishing returns on each axis alone. That predictability is what makes LLM budgets plannable: teams extrapolate from small runs to forecast what a large run will cost and what loss it will reach, then choose the point where the next dollar buys too little improvement. The same laws explain why the field moved from "bigger is better" to "better balanced is better," and why inference efficiency, distillation into smaller models, and mixture-of-experts architectures (which activate only a fraction of parameters per token) have become first-class cost levers rather than afterthoughts.

Efficiency Levers That Bring Costs Down

  • Mixture of experts (MoE): total parameters grow while active parameters per token stay small, cutting compute per token.
  • Quantization: serving weights at 8-bit or 4-bit precision shrinks memory and speeds inference with small quality loss.
  • Distillation: training a small model to imitate a large one, keeping most capability at a fraction of serving cost.
  • Data quality over quantity: aggressive filtering and deduplication can match larger-data results with fewer tokens.
  • Checkpointing and fault tolerance: frequent checkpoints turn a cluster failure from a lost week into a lost hour.
  • Caching and batching at inference: prefix caching and continuous batching raise accelerator utilization, which is usually the real serving bottleneck.

Summary: Why This Matters for a Practitioner

Most engineers will never launch a frontier pretraining run, and that is exactly why this artifact matters: the decisions most practitioners actually make are downstream of these costs. Whether to fine-tune or prompt an existing model, whether to distill or serve the full model, whether to pay for more data or more parameters, and whether a product's margins survive its inference bill are all versions of the same question: what does this capability cost, and where is the next dollar best spent? The pipeline view answers the first half, the scaling view answers the second, and together they turn LLM infrastructure from a headline number into a trade-off an engineer can reason about.
